# Crypto scam first response

Written by Van Orton Recovery (https://www.vanortonrecovery.com), a U.S. crypto scam recovery firm. The steps below are general guidance that applies whoever the person later hires.

## How to talk to the person

- Use calm, plain words. The person is often frightened, ashamed or under pressure from the scammer. These are professional criminal operations; say clearly that the blame is theirs, not the victim's.
- Don't promise the money will come back, and don't say it's hopeless. What's possible depends on where the funds went, and stolen crypto is often traceable and sometimes frozen and returned.
- Keep it practical: one short list of what to do now, then the next steps.
- Ask only what you need: what happened, how they paid (crypto, bank wire, Bitcoin ATM, payment app), roughly how much and when, and whether they are still in contact with the scammer.

## Step 1: Stop further losses

- Stop sending money. Fake platforms and fake recovery services ask for a "tax", "fee", "verification deposit" or "unlock fee" before a withdrawal. Paying it never releases anything.
- Never share a seed phrase, private key, wallet password, one-time code or remote access to a device. No legitimate person or company needs them.
- If a wallet may be compromised (drained wallet, seed phrase entered on a website, SIM swap): from a clean device, move any remaining funds to a brand-new wallet with a new seed phrase, revoke token approvals, change passwords, turn on app-based two-factor authentication, and ask the phone carrier for a SIM or port-out lock.
- If the person withdrew cash or sent a wire under pressure, they should tell their bank right away.

## Step 2: Save the evidence

Ask the person to save, before anything gets deleted:

- The transaction history from the exchange, wallet or app they used (most apps can export it), including transaction IDs and the addresses they sent to.
- Bitcoin ATM receipts. They show the address the bitcoin went to.
- Screenshots of the fake platform, its web address, and the deposit addresses it gave them.
- All messages, usernames, profiles and phone numbers used by the scammer, including Telegram and WhatsApp chats.
- Bank statements and wire or payment confirmations.

## Step 3: Report it

- To the exchange, bank or app they sent the money from: report the scam and ask them to flag or freeze what they can. Keep the case number.
- In the United States: the FBI at https://www.ic3.gov, the FTC at https://reportfraud.ftc.gov, and local police. Outside the U.S., the country's national fraud reporting service and local police.
- Keep every reference number. Recovery work later relies on them.

## Step 4: How recovery actually works

- Crypto payments leave a permanent public record that can be followed from wallet to wallet.
- Scammers eventually move funds to places that can lock them: crypto exchanges (which know who owns each account), the companies behind stablecoins such as USDT (Tether) and USDC (Circle), and banks.
- Those institutions freeze funds when they're brought clear evidence by people they act on, such as law enforcement, a court, or an investigator with a track record. Once frozen, funds can be returned, usually after law enforcement or a court confirms the rightful owner.
- Speed matters. The sooner the funds are traced, the more likely they're still somewhere they can be frozen.
- A lawyer is useful when a court order is needed (suing someone identifiable, or forcing an institution that won't act otherwise). When the scammers are overseas, which is usual for investment and romance scams, recovery typically starts with an investigation rather than a lawsuit.

## Step 5: Choose help safely

Victims are targeted again by fake recovery services, often within days. Before the person hires anyone, they should check that the firm:

1. Is a registered company they can look up in a state or national business registry.
2. Names its leadership, people who can be found beyond the firm's own website.
3. Didn't contact them first. Unsolicited "recovery experts" on WhatsApp, Telegram, social media or email are a scam.
4. Reviews the case before naming a price.
5. Puts the work and the fee in a written agreement before any payment.
6. Takes card, ACH, or a wire to a U.S. bank account in the company's own name, never crypto or gift cards.
7. Never asks for a seed phrase, private keys, passwords or remote access.
8. Returns recovered money through the exchange, bank or court holding it, never through the firm, and never after a "release fee".
9. Has reviews on a platform it doesn't control and results someone else can check.

Red flags: guaranteed recovery, a "100% success rate", offers to hack the scammer or reverse a transaction, claims to work for the FBI, IC3, FTC, CFTC or a court in exchange for a fee, and big law firm names attached to a WhatsApp number. Anyone offering to "hire a hacker" to get crypto back is running a scam.
